# Presenter script — Methods of Proof, Group 3

Cat explanations are optional. The human presenter can use these cues alone. Treat everyday conditionals as assumed premises, even when they are not real world guarantees.

## 1. Methods of Proof

**Purpose:** Set the goal. **Say:** “A proof needs a conclusion and reasons for every step leading to it.” **Cue:** Optional cat steps 1–2. **Ask/answer:** What besides a true answer? A justified chain. **Transition:** Meet the group.

## 2. Our Team

**Purpose:** Introduce the team. **Say:** “These eight members of Group 3 prepared the lesson.” **Cue:** Click **All members** to show all names, then optionally return to Carousel view. **Transition:** Define a proof.

## 3. Foundations

**Purpose:** Separate starting points, steps, and result. **Say:** “An axiom is accepted as a basic statement. A hypothesis is what this claim asks us to assume. A proof uses definitions, known results, and valid rules to reach a conclusion. A theorem is a claim with a proof; a fallacy is a faulty step.” **Cue:** Cat steps highlight Start, Justify, Establish. **Ask/answer:** Does an axiom create a hypothesis? No; the hypothesis comes from the claim. **Transition:** Read the symbols.

## 4. Logic Symbols and Statements

**Purpose:** Build a symbol key. **Say:** “A proposition is true or false. The letters p, q, and r stand for statements. Read not, and, or, implies, and therefore. Logical or permits either or both. In p implies q, p is the hypothesis and q the conclusion.” **Cue:** Cat steps 1–3; show the anytime symbol guide if useful. **Ask/answer:** Does square implies rectangle prove rectangle implies square? No. It also says nothing about cause. **Transition:** Use the symbols in rules.

## 5. Rules of Inference

**Purpose:** Introduce valid patterns. **Say:** “If the premises hold, a valid inference rule gives a conclusion that must follow.” **Cue:** Choose a rule link or advance in order. **Transition:** Start with Modus Ponens.

## 6. Modus Ponens

**Purpose:** Apply a conditional. **Say:** “Let p be snow and q be skiing. Given p implies q and p, q follows under those assumed premises.” **Cue:** Point to each given premise, ask for a prediction, then **Show conclusion** or cat step 3. **Answer:** q, skiing. **Transition:** An ‘and’ statement.

## 7. Simplification

**Purpose:** Separate conjuncts. **Say:** “Let p be freezing and q be raining. If p and q both hold, p follows and q follows separately.” **Cue:** Point to p ∧ q, predict, reveal. **Answer:** Either part on its own. **Transition:** Negate a result.

## 8. Modus Tollens

**Purpose:** Use a failed conclusion. **Say:** “Let p mean the angle is right and q mean it measures ninety degrees. If p implies q but q is false, p must be false.” **Cue:** Point to each premise, predict, reveal. **Answer:** Not p. **Transition:** Chain conditionals.

## 9. Hypothetical Syllogism

**Purpose:** Link implications. **Say:** “The shared q joins p implies q to q implies r. We infer p implies r under the assumed premises. The school example is about the logical form, not a grade guarantee.” **Cue:** Point to both occurrences of q, predict, reveal. **Answer:** p implies r. **Transition:** Eliminate an option.

## 10. Disjunctive Syllogism

**Purpose:** Use inclusive OR. **Say:** “At least one of p or q holds; both may hold. If p is ruled out, q remains.” **Cue:** Point to p ∨ q and ¬p, then reveal. **Answer:** q, burger in the example. **Transition:** Combine rules.

## 11. Building a Proof with Inference Rules

**Purpose:** Build a justified argument. **Say:** “Use one shape throughout: p means square, q rectangle, r four sides. The green lines are given. From p implies q and q implies r, derive p implies r by Hypothetical Syllogism. Then p and p implies r give r by Modus Ponens.” **Cue:** Ask for line 4; press **Next step**. Ask for line 5; press **Next step**. Previous step reverses a reveal. **Answers:** p → r, then r. **Transition:** Compare invalid steps.

## 12. Fallacies

**Purpose:** Explain three failures. **Say:** “Let p be rain and q wet road, assuming p implies q. A sprinkler gives p false and q true. That makes affirming the consequent’s premises true while its proposed p is false. It also defeats denying the antecedent: ¬p is true but proposed ¬q is false. Circular reasoning only repeats its claim.” **Cue:** Open each fallacy separately or use cat steps 1–4. Contrast the Modus Ponens line. **Answer:** Wet road does not prove rain; no rain does not prove dry road. **Transition:** Examine implication truth values.

## 13. Understanding an Implication

**Purpose:** Interpret all four cases. **Say:** “The conditional fails only with p true and q false. If p is false, this case has not violated the promise; it does not prove q. One row is a case, not a proof about every integer.” **Cue:** Show the table; toggle TT, TF, FT, FF. **Answer:** T, F, T, T for p → q. **Transition:** Introduce proof approaches.

## 14. Trivial, Vacuous, and Direct Proofs

**Purpose:** Distinguish three introductory approaches over integers. **Say:** “Trivial is a technical name: prove the conclusion independently. An integer square is nonnegative. Vacuous: the hypothesis cannot occur; an integer square cannot be negative. Direct: assume the hypothesis and derive the conclusion.” **Cue:** Select each card or cat step. **Answer:** Conclusion already true, hypothesis impossible, or justified chain. **Transition:** Work a direct proof.

## 15. Direct Proof Example

**Purpose:** Prove a universal divisibility claim. **Say:** “For every integer x, if 6 divides x, then 3 divides x. The goal is 3 divides x. The assumption gives x = 6k for an integer k. ‘Divides’ means an integer multiple.” **Cue:** Advance five steps individually. Name each displayed reason: definition, factor 6, regroup, 2k is an integer, definition again. **Ask/answer:** What if x is zero or negative? k may be zero or negative; the algebra still works. **Transition:** Try a parallel proof.

## 16. Guided Proof Practice

**Purpose:** Construct the argument. **Say:** “For every integer n, show that 4 divides n implies 2 divides n. Start from the assumption; type or draw your reasoning.” **Cue:** Wait; **Show hint** only if needed. **Show solution** separately after discussion. **Answer:** n = 4k = 2(2k), with k and 2k integers, so 2 divides n. Undo and Clear drawing affect strokes only. **Transition:** Check understanding.

## 17. Challenges

**Purpose:** Check rule, fallacy, and missing proof step. **Say:** “Choose an answer before checking. Explain why.” **Cue:** Collect responses, then **Check answers**; Retry resets them. **Answers:** 1 Modus Ponens; 2 invalid affirming the consequent; 3 2k is an integer, so 2 divides n. Unanswered items keep their answers hidden on screen. **Transition:** Summarize the process.

## 18. Conclusion

**Purpose:** Leave a repeatable process. **Say:** “State the claim, identify assumptions, justify every step, reach the result, and check that the reasoning covers the domain.” **Cue:** Cat steps highlight these parts. **Transition:** Invite questions.

## 19. Thank You

**Purpose:** Close and invite review. **Say:** “Thank you. Which step should we revisit?” **Cue:** **Prove It Again** returns to slide 1; the overview jumps to any slide. Local language jokes are optional in the tools menu. **Answer:** Revisit the requested step or example.
