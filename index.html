The Foundations of a Mathematical System

Rules of Inference

Rules of Inference

Modus Ponens or Law of Detachment

Form

pp → q
Therefore
q

When an implication and its hypothesis are true, its conclusion follows.

Example

“If it snows today, then we will go skiing.”

“It is snowing today.”

Therefore, “We will go skiing.”

Rules of Inference

Simplification

Form

p ∧ q
Therefore
p

From a statement joined by “and,” either part may be inferred.

Example

“It is below freezing and raining now.”

Therefore, “It is below freezing now.”

Rules of Inference

Modus Tollens

Form

p → q¬q
Therefore
¬p

If the promised result does not hold, the hypothesis cannot hold.

Example

“If an angle is right, then its measure is 90°.”

“This angle's measure is not 90°.”

Therefore, the angle is not a right angle.

Rules of Inference

Hypothetical Syllogism

Form

p → qq → r
Therefore
p → r

Two implications can be chained through a shared statement.

Example

“If you study hard, then you will pass the exam.”

“If you pass the exam, then you will get a good grade.”

Therefore, if you study hard, you will get a good grade.

Rules of Inference

Disjunctive Syllogism

Form

p ∨ q¬p
Therefore
q

When one of two possibilities is ruled out, the other remains.

Example

“I will order a pizza or a burger.”

“I did not order pizza.”

Therefore, “I ordered a burger.”

Fallacies

1. Affirming the Conclusion

From p → q and q, it does not follow that p. Let p be false and q true.

2. Denying the Hypothesis

From p → q and ¬p, it does not follow that ¬q. Again, p can be false while q is true.

3. Begging the Question

Circular reasoning uses the truth of the very statement a proof is trying to establish.

Methods of Proof of an Implication

Many mathematical problems take the form “If p, then q.” We write this as p → q.

To prove it directly, assume p is true and show that q follows.

When p is false, the implication is true regardless of q. Try the truth values to see the one case that makes p → q false.

Types of Proof

1Trivial Proof

If q is true regardless of p, then p → q is true. Example: A ⊆ A ∪ B for any sets A and B.

2Vacuous Proof

If p is false, p → q is true regardless of q. An impossible hypothesis is enough for a vacuous proof.

3Direct Proof

Assume p and build a logical chain: p → statement 1 → statement 2 → … → q.

Challenges

1. It is raining and cold. What follows by simplification?

It is raining. “It is cold” also follows.

2. If p → q and ¬q, what can you conclude?

¬p, by Modus Tollens.

3. Is a proof valid if it assumes its own conclusion?

No. That is circular reasoning, also called begging the question.

Conclusion

  • A mathematical proof is a chain of justified statements leading to a conclusion.
  • Rules of inference preserve valid reasoning; fallacies only imitate it.
  • For p → q, choose a proof method that fits the hypothesis and the conclusion.
