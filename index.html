Foundations

A proof shows why a claim follows from accepted starting points.

Start

Axioms are accepted basics. A hypothesis is the assumption in this claim.

Justify

Use definitions, earlier results, and valid inference rules for each step.

Establish

The result is the conclusion. A theorem is a claim with a proof.

A fallacy is a step that looks persuasive but does not follow.

Logic Symbols and Statements

A proposition is a statement that is true or false. Letters p, q, r stand for statements.

¬pnot p: “not raining”
p ∧ qp and q: “square and blue”
p ∨ qp or q: “tea or juice”—either or both
p → qif p, then q: “if square, then rectangle”
∴ qtherefore q: marks a conclusion

Rules of Inference

In everyday examples, treat each “if … then …” sentence as an assumed premise.

Rules of Inference

Modus Ponens or Law of Detachment

p = it snows today; q = we go skiing.

Form

pp → q
Derived conclusion
q

Assuming both premises, p gives the condition that triggers p → q.

When an implication and its hypothesis are true, its conclusion follows.

Example

“If it snows today, then we will go skiing.”

“It is snowing today.”

Conclusion: Therefore, “We will go skiing.”

Rules of Inference

Simplification

p = it is below freezing; q = it is raining.

Form

p ∧ q
Derived conclusion
p (or q separately)

Both parts of p ∧ q hold, so p follows and q also follows separately.

From a statement joined by “and,” either part may be inferred.

Example

“It is below freezing and raining now.”

Conclusion: Therefore, “It is below freezing now.” Separately, “It is raining now.”

Rules of Inference

Modus Tollens

p = this angle is right; q = its measure is 90°.

Form

p → q¬q
Derived conclusion
¬p

If p held, q would hold. Since q does not hold, p cannot hold.

If the promised result does not hold, the hypothesis cannot hold.

Example

“If an angle is right, then its measure is 90°.”

“This angle's measure is not 90°.”

Conclusion: Therefore, the angle is not a right angle.

Rules of Inference

Hypothetical Syllogism

p = you study hard; q = you pass; r = you get a good grade.

Form

p → qq → r
Derived conclusion
p → r

Under the assumed premises, the shared q links p to r.

This is a logical result of the assumed premises, not a guarantee about real grades.

Example

“If you study hard, then you will pass the exam.”

“If you pass the exam, then you will get a good grade.”

Conclusion: Therefore, if you study hard, you will get a good grade.

Rules of Inference

Disjunctive Syllogism

p = I ordered pizza; q = I ordered a burger.

Form

p ∨ q¬p
Derived conclusion
q

At least one option is true. Ruling out p leaves q.

When one of two possibilities is ruled out, the other remains.

Assumed premises

“I ordered a pizza or a burger.”

“I did not order pizza.”

Conclusion: Therefore, “I ordered a burger.”

Fallacies

Compare each proposed conclusion with what the premises actually allow.

Affirming the consequent

Invalid: p → q; q; ∴ p. Let p = rain and q = wet road. The implication can be true when p is false and q is true: a sprinkler wets the road. Both premises are true, but “it rained” is false.

Denying the antecedent

Invalid: p → q; ¬p; ∴ ¬q. With no rain and a wet road, the assumed implication and ¬p are true, yet “the road is not wet” is false. A sprinkler explains the water.

Circular reasoning

“This claim is true because the claim is true” merely repeats the claim. It supplies no independent reason.

Valid Modus Ponens: p → q; p; ∴ q. The second premise is p, not q.

Understanding an Implication

p → q means “if p, then q.” Here p is the hypothesis and q is the conclusion.

The conditional fails only when p is true and q is false.

When p is false, this case violates no requirement; it does not prove q true.

A truth-table row is one case. A proof of “for every integer” must cover every integer.

Types of Proof

Three introductory approaches over the integers:

1Trivial Proof

Over the integers: “If n is odd, then n² ≥ 0.” Prove the conclusion independently: every integer square is nonnegative. “Trivial” is a technical term.

2Vacuous Proof

Over the integers: “If n² < 0, then n is odd.” Show the hypothesis cannot hold in this domain: an integer square is never negative.

3Direct Proof

Assume the hypothesis p and justify each step until the conclusion q follows.

For every integer x, if 6 divides x, then 3 divides x.

Goal: 3 divides x. Assume: 6 divides x, so x = 6k for an integer k. a | b means b = at for some integer t.

Guided Proof Practice

For every integer n, if 4 divides n, then 2 divides n.

Hint: Start with n = 4k for an integer k. Can you write it as 2 times an integer?

Solution: n = 4k = 2(2k). Since k is an integer, 2k is an integer. Therefore 2 divides n.

Challenges

1. p → q and p are given. Which rule gives q?

Answer: Modus Ponens uses the hypothesis p.

2. p → q and q are given. Is “therefore p” valid?

Answer: No. This affirms the consequent; q may have another cause.

3. n = 4k = 2(2k). What completes the proof?

Answer: 2k is an integer, so 2 divides n.

Conclusion

  • State the claim and identify its assumptions.
  • Justify each step using a definition, known fact, or valid inference rule.
  • Reach the conclusion, then check that the reasoning covers every required case.
